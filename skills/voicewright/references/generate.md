# Generating lines

- Line list: JSON or CSV of `id`, `voice`, `text`, `say`, `direction`, `emotion`. `text` is for subtitles, `say` is for the engine. Name clips `<voice>_<id>_t<take>.wav`. A game dialogue file is converted to this list, never edited.
- Pronunciation: respell in `say` to a spelling with one reading ("bough" for a ship's bow). Speech-to-text writes words, not sounds, so it passes the wrong homograph; check the vowel with a phoneme recogniser or by ear.
- Normalise: in `say`, write numbers, dates, money, abbreviations, symbols and web addresses as the words to be spoken. An unusual string is a first suspect when a line fails.
- Writing `say` for the ear: punctuation in `text` is for the eye. Put a pause (dash or ellipsis) just before the word that matters, one or two a line at most. Render with and without on one seed and keep what the ear prefers; engines read marks differently. Split a long `say` by thought, not by sentence: a breath point (comma, dash or chunk cut) at each change of thought and none inside one; a short thought stays short. On a line built round one image word (cold, blood), direction may name that word to land; one per line at most.
- Chunks: engines drift and speed up on long text and break on very short lines. Stay under about 20 s or 350 characters a chunk; pad a one-word line inside a phrase and cut it out. Cut at sentence ends, or at phrase ends inside a long sentence. A chunk rendered alone loses the sentences around it, so listen across the joins for resets in pitch and pace.
- References: 5-30 s of dry speech, one clear delivery, nothing under it; room sound in a reference carries into every take. In-context clones copy the reference's delivery; for a shout use embedding-only mode (Qwen3 `x_vector`) and one reference per emotion.
- Direction: one sentence each for the event the line answers (one specific thing just said or done, not a mood), who it is said to, and the thought under the words. Long director's notes are a known cause of voice drift, so keep identity out of them. Add, when it helps, the tactic (what the speaker wants the listener to do) and why these words over plainer ones. Mark each turn in the line (but, if, though, a punctuation turn) and say what changes there; a take with the right mood and no change at the turns is a generalised read. A flat or wrong read has three levers, where the engine exposes them: the reference (a clip with that delivery), the text (wording that implies the feeling) and the seed. A requested emotion may simply be ignored, so judge by ear.
- Dialogue: render each line alone with frozen voices and join, unless a test proves a native multi-speaker engine holds its voices. Timing lives in the join: set each gap by hand, short for a picked-up cue, about a second for a beat, overlap or a cut-off tail for an interruption. Start from each speaker's `turn_gap`: latch near zero, short a fraction of a second, long a clear pause. Render back-channels ("mm", "right", an echoed word) as short padded clips and lay them quietly under the speaker, who keeps talking. A cooperative overlap lets both lines finish; a takeover cuts the first speaker's clip mid-word and drops its tail (book rule, unverified).
- Batches: use the idle host; on a busy shared GPU queue behind it and wait. `engines/qwen3.py jobs.json` runs a resumable batch.
- Review: `VW review`; note heard or unheard for each clip.
- Retry notes: name one move, not a mood: no stress on the phrase, the opposite feeling, a new thought under the line, a pause at one named place, a pause just before the key word, one drawn-out word against a clipped clause, an earnest contour that starts high and falls, less tune and more point (sing-song), let it sit lower (thin), drop out of the nose (nasal). For an important line render three takes with three different thoughts.
- First test by fault: a skipped or repeated word or garbled stretch points at alignment, so diff a transcript against the text, shorten the chunk, simplify the text, try a new seed. Hoarse, metallic or pitch-breaking sound points at the waveform stage, so rewording is less likely to help: try another seed or engine.
- Take faults by name, heard not measured: sing-song (a repeated rise and fall that describes the feeling instead of meaning it), head monotone (high, flat), strained (gritty, hoarse), thin (light or strident, no chest), flat-rich bass (low, no upper inflection), nasal (loud, nuance ironed out; a direction rarely fixes it, recast), generalised (one mood, no reason), held pitch (vowels sustained like singing). In a scene, three or more lines of one voice at the same pace, pitch and level are a fault: contrast carries rhythm and turns. Ask on a review page whether anything changed at the marked turn.
- Direction, `say` writing, timing and retry notes come from human voice-acting craft (Alburger), the fault split from a 2023 textbook on older models (Tan), thought-splitting, turns, tactics and the fault names from Linklater, Freeing the Natural Voice (2006, Workdays 4 and 7, sections 7, 9, 35, 37). None has been tested on current engines: they pick what to try, and a blind listen decides.
